iOS vs Android vs cross-platform: which costs more?

Platform choice is one of the biggest cost levers in mobile app development — often bigger than any single feature decision.

Approach Typical cost impact Best for
Native, iOS only Baseline (1×) Launching Apple-first, or testing product-market fit on one platform.
Native, Android only Similar to iOS-only for equivalent scope Markets or user bases where Android dominates.
Native, both platforms ~1.6–1.8× a single native build Performance-critical apps or heavy platform-specific APIs (camera, biometrics, AR).
Cross-platform (Flutter / React Native) ~1.5–1.7× a single native build, but covers both platforms Most apps — one codebase, faster time-to-market, still feels native to users.

Cross-platform frameworks have narrowed the performance gap with native apps considerably, but very graphics-heavy apps (games, AR) or apps leaning hard on platform-specific hardware still often justify going native.

What drives mobile app development cost

Driver What it affects Typical cost impact
Backend & APIs Accounts, real-time data, payments, third-party integrations. Simple apps with no backend run leanest; real-time or payment features add the most engineering time.
App Store / Play Store requirements Platform guidelines, review process, submission prep. Usually 3–10% of build cost; more for regulated categories like finance or health.
Device & OS fragmentation Screen sizes, OS versions, older devices. Android testing typically costs more than iOS due to wider device variety.
Offline functionality Local storage and data-sync/conflict handling. Adds meaningfully if the app must work reliably with no connection.
Push notifications & real-time features Messaging, live updates, background sync. Common add-on; moderate cost increase depending on volume and complexity.

Features that typically increase app cost

Check these mentally — each one adds design, engineering, and testing time.

  • Accounts & auth: sign-up, login, password resets, social sign-in.
  • Payments & subscriptions: in-app purchases, billing, receipt validation.
  • Push notifications: transactional and marketing messaging.
  • Offline mode: local caching and sync when connectivity returns.
  • Device hardware: camera, GPS/location, Bluetooth, biometrics.
  • Admin tools: content management, user support, analytics dashboards.

No-code app builders: a cheaper starting point?

If your budget doesn't stretch to custom development yet, a no-code builder like Adalo↗ can get a simple app in front of real users for a fraction of the cost — useful for validating an idea before committing to a custom build. Mobile app development cost: common questions

How much does it cost to build a mobile app?

Most mobile apps cost $20,000–$80,000 for a cross-platform MVP with moderate complexity. Simple single-platform apps built by a freelancer can start around $8,000–$30,000, while production-ready apps with native iOS and Android builds, complex backends, or heavy integrations often exceed $150,000. Use the estimator above to adjust for your specific platform and scope.

Is it cheaper to build for iOS or Android first?

Building for a single platform first is typically the cheapest way to launch, since you avoid the cost of supporting two codebases or a cross-platform framework. iOS and Android development costs are usually similar for equivalent scope, though Android often needs more device and OS-version testing due to the wider range of hardware in use.

Should I use a no-code app builder instead of custom development?

No-code builders can be a good fit for simple MVPs, internal tools, or validating an idea before investing in custom development, often for a fraction of the cost. They become limiting once you need complex backend logic, heavy third-party integrations, or platform-specific performance, which is when custom development becomes worth the investment.

What ongoing costs come after launching a mobile app?

Budget for the Apple Developer Program ($99/year) and a one-time $25 Google Play registration fee, plus backend hosting, push notification services, and crash monitoring if your app uses them. Most apps also need periodic updates to stay compatible with new iOS and Android releases.
